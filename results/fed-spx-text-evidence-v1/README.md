# FOMC minutes certify 107 of 110 statements

The FOMC's own meeting minutes are the source that can establish an exact statement version was public by its text cutoff. The minutes reproduce the approved statement right after an explicit release-time phrase such as "to be released at 2:15 p.m." or "for release at 2:00 p.m." ([FOMC minutes Jan 29–30, 2008](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20080130.htm); [FOMC minutes Mar 15, 2020](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20200315.htm)). An official-record pass executed this session on all 110 price-eligible statement rows bore this out: **107 rows have proof, 89 in training and 18 in validation, and the same 89 and 18 rows survive at both the 20- and 30-minute horizons**. Price filters do not limit the cohort. Every in-session statement has 35 input bars and complete targets, and shortening the buffer or the input recovers nothing. The limits are the number of statements, the reserved test period and seven releases outside the cash session. Wayback captures help as corroboration, but none predates a release and several statements have no same-day capture at all. Timestamped newswire archives are the best independent source, but nobody has checked them yet. The recommendation is to adopt a two-part ledger rule: a minutes fingerprint plus a minutes release time no later than the cutoff. The model should read only the fingerprinted statement body, not the whole press-release page. Before adding any release type, run the matched 89/18 comparison. FOMC minutes are the natural next type, but they need their own version proof, and even with them the validation set would stay small.

Throughout, three kinds of claim are kept apart. **Executed** results were run in this session against federalreserve.gov and web.archive.org; the raw files and ledgers are under `downloads/fomc-statement-evidence-pilot/` (Git-ignored) in the worktree of branch `adaeze/fed-spx-compact-endpoints` (PR #46 head c8c89b5). **Documented** facts come from cited sources. **Proposals** are labelled as such.

## Minutes, not web archives, tie the wording to the release clock

**Documented.** The minutes record the Committee's vote on the statement and give the time it was to be released. The wording changed over the years. In 2008–2011 it read "statement below to be released at [time]" ([Oct 28–29, 2008 minutes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20081029.htm); [Apr 26–27, 2011 minutes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20110427.htm)). By 2020 it read "statement below for release at [time]," and that form also covers unscheduled actions such as the 10:00 a.m. statement of March 3, 2020, which is recorded inside the March 15, 2020 minutes ([Mar 15, 2020 minutes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20200315.htm)). Release clocks moved in steps. Statements came at 2:15 p.m. until 2011. From April 2011, press-conference meetings released at 12:30 p.m. ([Apr 26–27, 2011 minutes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20110427.htm)), while other meetings stayed at 2:15 p.m., as the executed pass confirms for August 2012. On March 13, 2013 the Board moved all scheduled statements to 2 p.m. ([Board press release, Mar 13, 2013](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20130313a.htm)). The minutes appear about three weeks after the decision ([FOMC historical materials](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc_historical.htm)). They are therefore an after-the-fact record of the approved text and its scheduled time, not a log of what the web server showed at 2:00 p.m.

Two documented weak spots in the minutes do not touch this cohort. The January 21, 2008 call approved only "text below for inclusion in the statement," for release at 8:30 a.m. The October 7, 2008 call gave no release time for the coordinated cut, which the press release shows went out at 7:00 a.m. ([Jan 2008 minutes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20080130.htm); [Oct 8, 2008 release](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20081008a.htm)). Both releases came before the cash open, so both drop out at the session filter whatever the text evidence shows.

Federal Register and GovInfo were not researched. The FOMC transcripts arrive about five years late and record the deliberation, not a release timestamp ([FOMC historical materials](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc_historical.htm)). Public text corpora such as the Hugging Face FOMC statement set are scraped from today's site, so they document the current text, not the version public at release ([Hugging Face dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Coding-Fish/fomc-statements/viewer/default/train?p=1)).

**Executed.** The Wayback pilot confirms that archives corroborate a statement but do not prove it was public at release. Across eight statements, **no capture anywhere preceded a release**. Six had a same-day capture, from 1.1 minutes after release (July 28, 2021) to 403 minutes after (November 3, 2010), and each matched today's page. January 30, 2008 and July 31, 2013 had no same-day capture at all. Querying both the current `/newsevents/pressreleases/monetaryYYYYMMDDa.htm` path and the legacy `/newsevents/press/monetary/YYYYMMDDa.htm` path mattered, because older captures can be indexed only under the legacy path. That fits the inference in the notes that an earlier audit's zero verifications came from blocked requests and single-URL lookups, not from missing captures. Since September 2026 the Internet Archive has answered high-volume automated traffic with HTTP 429 blocks ([IA blog, Sep 15, 2026](https://blog.archive.org/2026/09/15/an-update-on-wayback-machine-access/)). A widely used client sets about 0.4 CDX requests per second at what it says is 80% of the hard limit ([EDGI wayback client](https://wayback.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_modules/wayback/_client.html)). For these reasons Wayback should stay optional corroboration, not a gate.

## 89 training and 18 validation rows survive at both horizons

**Executed.** The funnel below starts from the 160 catalog rows filed as `stmt_`: 157 statements plus 3 other policy releases. The price data are the frozen five-minute cash S&P 500 file `SP500.csv`, SHA-256 `6a873b1d72d27f9188c6bf12acb756775916be8ed9ff42d9e9dd98028f68de2a`. It matches the frozen hash, and its coverage ends April 17, 2024. Catalog and alignment hashes are enforced by `data/fed_spx_endpoints.INPUT_SHA256`. In each row the last of the 35 input bars ends at the release time, and the forecast origin is release plus 5 minutes. The text cutoff equals the release time.

The train/validation split at step 3b is worked back from the step 4 losses. The seven off-session releases were January 22, 2008 (8:30 a.m.), October 8, 2008 (7:00 a.m.), Sunday May 9, 2010 (9:15 p.m.), Sunday March 15, 2020 (5:00 p.m.), March 19, 2020, March 23, 2020 (8:00 a.m.) and March 31, 2020. Off-session releases have no cash bars at the origin. Including them would change the target, for example to the return from the next open, and that is a different task. The final cohort is 89 training rows on 89 distinct dates and release instants, and 18 validation rows on 18 distinct dates and release instants. The rows are identical at both horizons, so a 20-versus-30-minute comparison uses the same rows in both arms.

Release clocks from the minutes are 67 at 2:00 p.m., 33 at 2:15 p.m., 8 at 12:30 p.m. and 1 at 10:00 a.m., all at or before the text cutoff. That totals 109. The 110th row, October 11, 2019, has no release time in its minutes. The 10:00 a.m. row is March 3, 2020, and it is already in the price-eligible population. One research note claimed a release must come at or after 12:25 p.m. so that all 35 bars fall in the same session. **That claim is wrong for this project.** The existing alignment already lets the 35-bar input reach into the previous session. Morning releases are therefore eligible as long as both arms see the same history and the overnight gap is handled the same way.

The sensitivity checks settle where the bottleneck is. **Buffers of 0 to 4 minutes recover no rows, and neither do 12- or 24-bar inputs.** The five-minute processing buffer stays, and it costs nothing in this cohort. The count is limited by the roughly eight scheduled statements a year, the 21 rows reserved for test and the 7 off-session releases.

## Three statements fail the text check, and 17 posted pages carry extra text

**Executed.** Three of the 110 rows lack proof, and each fails differently. The April 25, 2012 statement (training, 12:30 p.m.) reads "coming quarters then to pick up" in the minutes, but "coming quarters and then to pick up" on the posted page and in a Wayback capture 61 minutes after release. The August 1, 2012 statement (training, 2:15 p.m.) differs only by "ex-tend" in the minutes against "extend" on the page, and its earliest capture is about 29 hours late. That looks like a hyphenation artifact in the minutes, but the strict rule cannot tell. The October 11, 2019 notation-vote release (validation, catalog type `fomc_other_policy_release`) is reproduced in the October 30 minutes without a release time. Its page says "For release at 11:00 a.m. EDT." A same-day capture 6.5 hours after release differs from today's page only by a later-added FAQ link line. **The catalog hash therefore identifies a page version that did not exist at release.** This is a concrete instance of the problem the exact-version rule exists to prevent.

The full pass found two more facts that shape the rule. First, all 110 current pages still match the catalog hashes, but **17 posted pages end with extra paragraphs the minutes do not cover**, such as discount-rate actions and New York Fed statements. In the pilot, January 30, 2008 added a discount-rate paragraph and November 3, 2010 added a New York Fed paragraph. Those paragraphs have no official release-time record of their own in this pass. Second, a footnote in the December 16, 2020 minutes says the approved statement had a drafting error. A corrected version was approved by notation vote shortly after the meeting for release at 2:00 p.m. The minutes reproduce the corrected text, which matches the posted page. So the minutes recorded the version that went public, not the first draft voted on.

**Documented.** No Fed erratum or reissue of a posted 2008–2024 statement turned up in the research. That absence is weak evidence, because federalreserve.gov pages carry only a day-level "Last Update" date and no change log ([Oct 8, 2008 page](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20081008a.htm)). The October 2019 FAQ line shows that pages do change after release, and nothing on the page records it.

## A two-part ledger rule keeps the exact-version promise

**Proposal.** A statement row enters the text arm only if two conditions hold, and the model then reads only the fingerprinted body. The **text condition** is that an official record (the minutes or a listed override) reproduces a statement body whose SHA-256 fingerprint, after documented normalization, equals the matching body on the posted page. The **time condition** is that the same record gives a release time at or before the text cutoff, and that this time agrees with the page's "For release at" line. Extra trailing paragraphs stay out of the input until they get their own proof. A Wayback capture or wire record is logged as corroboration. If it disagrees with the fingerprint, the row is flagged, never silently accepted. This follows Adaeze's guidance that an official record reproducing the final statement and tying it to its release time is enough, without a Wayback copy. Under this rule, rows that fail are dropped, not repaired with today's page.

The ledger should store one record per row, so the train/validation filters and the comparison can be audited.

| Ledger field | Content |
|---|---|
| `event_id`, `split` | Catalog ID and split |
| `proof_source` | Minutes URL, or override entry for June 2008 (PDF-only minutes), March 3, 2020 (inside March 15 minutes) and October 11, 2019 |
| `release_time_record` | Exact phrase and clock from the minutes |
| `release_time_page` | "For release at" line from today's page |
| `fingerprint` | SHA-256 of the normalized minutes-approved body |
| `page_hash_match`, `extra_paragraphs` | Catalog hash check; flag and count of uncovered trailing text |
| `corroboration` | Optional Wayback CDX timestamp, digest and `id_` fetch hash, or wire record ID and timestamp |
| `decision`, `reason` | eligible / lacking proof, with the mismatch quoted |
| `fetched_at`, `script_sha256` | Retrieval time and tool hash |

The full hashes of the last two scripts were not recorded in the session notes. Normalization covers ¼ to 1/4, U+2011 hyphens to ASCII, and removal of footnote back-links, and the minutes parser handles blockquote, paragraph and PDF layouts.

## Run the matched comparison before widening the event set

**Proposal, in order.** First, commit the scripts, overrides and ledger once push access works. Push and PR #46 updates were blocked in this session, the Team Drive evidence folder was not reachable, and no training was run. Local GPU work and exports need Adaeze's confirmation. Second, run the matched experiment. Refit the price-only CNN on the same 89 training rows and evaluate both arms on the same 18 validation rows, at 20 and 30 minutes. The existing price-only reference uses 978 training and 207 validation rows across all Fed release types, so it is not a fair comparison. Third, set expectations. **Eighteen validation events cannot resolve differences of the size seen so far.** That was about 0.03 bp in MAE at 20 minutes, with intervals of about ±0.2 bp. The experiment should be read as a pipeline check, not as evidence about text value.

Recovering the three failed rows is cheap but small, adding at most 2 training rows and 1 validation row. A timestamped wire record from Dow Jones or Reuters in Factiva, or LSEG MRN with its millisecond version chain, would show which April 2012 wording went out at 12:30 p.m. and whether "ex-tend" was ever public ([LSEG MRN guide](https://developers.lseg.com/content/dam/devportal/api-families/elektron/elektron-sdk-c/documentation/development/rtnews_mrn.pdf)). Since late 2013 the lockup has transmitted over Fed-controlled data lines ([AP via MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2013/10/25/fed-announces-enhanced-news-security-procedures)), so wire timestamps after that date reflect real transmission. October 11, 2019 needs a design call either way. Its time comes from the page, not the minutes, and it is a different release type from a post-meeting statement.

Of the other release types, **FOMC minutes come first**. **Executed:** 87 training and 17 validation minutes rows at 2:00 p.m. already pass the price filters. Minutes come from the same publisher and URL patterns, and the FOMC set the three-weeks-later schedule in December 2004, with the first expedited minutes released at 2 p.m. on January 4, 2005 ([Atlanta Fed macroblog](https://www.atlantafed.org:443/blogs/macroblog/2004/12/15/news-that-didnt-make-much-of-a-splash-but-maybe-should-have)). The catch is that no later official record reproduces the minutes the way minutes reproduce statements, so (inference) their proof has to come from same-day archive captures or wire copies. The minutes lockup has failed at least once: on July 6, 2016 a technical error let one media organization send embargoed minutes early ([Board release, Jul 6, 2016](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20160706a.htm)). That date needs an earlier effective release time or exclusion. **The Beige Book comes second.** It is published eight times a year with a per-year archive ([Beige Book FAQs](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/beige-book-faqs.htm); [Beige Book archive](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/beige-book-archive.htm)). No fetched source confirmed its time of day, and its funnel has not been run. The note's estimate of about 130 releases from 2008 to April 2024 includes the reserved test years.

Private 10:00 a.m. releases are price-eligible under this repo's alignment, but they should wait. University of Michigan sentiment went to paying clients at 9:54:58 a.m. until July 2013, so its public time was not its first public time ([CNN Money, Jun 2013](https://money.cnn.com/2013/06/18/investing/high-speed-trading/index.html); [CNN Money, Jul 2013](https://money.cnn.com/2013/07/08/investing/thomson-reuters-consumer-sentiment/index.html)). ISM corrected its May 2014 manufacturing report after release ([PR Newswire correction](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/may-2014-manufacturing-ism-report-on-business-correction-261547971.html)) and moved its history behind a subscription ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/institute-for-supply-management-to-offer-subscription-access-to-historical-and-recent-ism-manufacturing-and-non-manufacturing-report-on-business-starting-march-1-245203881.html)). Each new type also changes the task: minutes come three weeks after the decision, and the Beige Book about two weeks before a meeting ([Wikipedia: Beige Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beige_Book)). Any pooled experiment should carry a release-type indicator and report results by type.

## Conclusion

Exact-version proof turns out to be the easy part for FOMC statements. The Committee's own minutes certify 97% of the price-eligible rows, and Wayback was never needed as a gate. The real constraints are sample size and the page itself. The current press-release page is not a safe stand-in for the released text: it can gain lines after release, and 17 pages mix in paragraphs with no proof. The fingerprinted minutes body should be the model input and the unit of record.

The case for text features cannot be settled with 18 validation events, and adding minutes would raise that to at most 35, even if every price-eligible minutes row passed its own proof. The value of the 89/18 experiment is a verified, hash-locked pipeline that any new release type can reuse. Each addition should pass the same two-part rule, with its own time record, before any row enters training.
